A whipped cream dispenser does one simple thing: it dissolves gas into a liquid under pressure, then releases it as a foam. Whipped cream is just the most famous result. Once you see it as a tool for making things light and airy, a lot of other doors open.

One rule applies to everything below: **whatever goes into the dispenser has to be completely smooth.** Strain mixtures through a fine-mesh sieve before you fill. Seeds, zest, and undissolved sugar are what clog a nozzle.

New to the dispenser? Start with our [beginner's guide](/blog/how-to-use-a-whipped-cream-dispenser/) — it covers filling, charging, and cleaning.

## 1. Flavored whipped creams

The easiest upgrade. Swap the vanilla in a basic whipped cream for something with more personality:

- **Maple:** 2 tablespoons of maple syrup in place of the sugar. Perfect on pancakes and pie.
- **Mocha:** 1 tablespoon of cocoa powder and 1 teaspoon of instant espresso, whisked into the cream until fully dissolved.
- **Bourbon vanilla:** a tablespoon of bourbon alongside the vanilla, for grown-up desserts.
- **Cinnamon:** half a teaspoon of ground cinnamon — whisk well and strain.

## 2. Chocolate mousse in minutes

Traditional mousse means whipping, folding, and hoping. The dispenser version skips all of it. Gently warm one cup of heavy cream and stir in about 3 ounces of chopped dark chocolate until it's fully melted and smooth. Sweeten to taste, then **chill the mixture completely** — a few hours, or overnight. Strain it into the dispenser, charge, shake, and dispense straight into glasses. The texture is lighter than classic mousse and it's ready the moment guests sit down.

## 3. Coffee-shop cold foam

That sweet, velvety layer on top of an iced coffee is easy to make at home. Combine half a cup of heavy cream, a quarter cup of milk, and 1–2 tablespoons of vanilla syrup in the dispenser. Charge it, shake just two or three times — you want it pourable, not stiff — and float it over cold brew or an iced latte. Try caramel or brown sugar syrup once you have the ratio you like.

## 4. Rapid infusions for cocktails

This is the trick bartenders love. Pressure pushes liquid into the pores of whatever aromatics are in the bottle; when the pressure is released, the liquid rushes back out carrying flavor with it. What normally takes days takes minutes.

Add a cup of spirit and your aromatics to the dispenser — jalapeño slices in tequila, fresh basil in gin, orange peel and cacao nibs in bourbon. Charge it, swirl gently for about a minute, then let it rest for another minute or two. Holding the dispenser **upright**, with a cup over the nozzle to catch any spray, release the gas slowly. Wait for the bubbling to settle, open it up, and strain. Start with short infusion times and taste as you go — strong flavors like chili come through fast.

## 5. Savory foams and toppings

Restaurants use dispensers on the savory side just as much as the sweet. A few places to start:

- **Herbed cream** for soups: heavy cream with a pinch of salt and finely strained herb oil, dispensed over tomato or squash soup.
- **Horseradish cream** for roast beef: cream, prepared horseradish (strained), salt, and a squeeze of lemon.
- **Whipped feta or goat cheese:** blend the cheese with cream until perfectly smooth, strain, and dispense onto crostini.

## Keep experimenting

The pattern is always the same: smooth liquid, enough fat or body to hold the air, well chilled, strained, charged. Once you've got that down, the dispenser earns a permanent spot on the counter — and you'll start looking at recipes wondering, *could I make that lighter?*
